About

Dr Sam Nnorom

A research methods and statistics PhD who evaluates programs for a living. He went looking for a way to prepare his own son for gifted screening, could not find one whose numbers he believed, and built it.

  • PhD, Research Methods & Statistics
  • MPH, Epidemiology
  • Program evaluation
  • Founder, The Gifted League

Dr Sam Chijioke Nnorom holds a PhD in Research Methods and Statistics from the University of Denver and an MPH in Public Health Epidemiology from Liberty University. He serves as adjunct faculty at the University of Denver’s Graduate School of Social Work, where he teaches and coordinates graduate research and evaluation courses and oversees a team of senior research and data analysts on a federally funded initiative.

His work sits where applied statistics meets public consequence: program evaluation, causal-comparative design, advanced statistical modelling, quantile regression, mortality analysis, health disparities and policy evaluation, built in R, SAS, SQL, Tableau and Power BI. Across more than a decade in higher education, public health and organisational analytics he has held positions with the Georgia Department of Public Health and the Colorado Department of Human Services.

Away from the data, he is an amateur chef with a standing interest in the flavours of authentic Nigerian cooking.

Origin

It started with one child and one deadline.

The Gifted League began as a study aid for a single first grader named Samuel, built by his father in the months before gifted screening. The options on the market were a subscription library of worksheets, a phone number for a tutor, and a great deal of confident marketing about acceptance rates. None of it answered the question a parent actually has, which is: what specifically is my child going to be asked to do, and is he ready to do it?

That question is a measurement problem, and measurement problems are the day job. So the first version was small and specific. Ten worlds, each mapped to a real diagnostic domain and a real state standards strand, so that a score referred to something. Everything read aloud, because a six year old who cannot yet decode a question independently is being measured on the wrong construct. And a game at the end that had to be earned, because the alternative was a parent standing over a child every evening.

It worked well enough on one child that it became worth building properly for everyone else’s. The first commit landed on 20 July 2026. Kindergarten through fifth grade, the Mind Gym, Mock Test Day and the printable packs followed over the next three weeks.

The discipline

What an evaluator brings to a children’s app.

Mostly, a refusal to accept a number that has not been checked, and a habit of naming what an instrument can and cannot measure.

Item quality

Every question is a measurement

Distractors are built so that guessing on shape fails: the longest option and the shortest option are both wrong, numeric choices bracket the key rather than sitting all above or all below it, and any two options that would render identically on screen are removed before a question is ever shown.

Construct validity

A score has to refer to something

Each world states its diagnostic domain and standards strand in the parent panel, and the app reports mathematics and reading ceilings separately because they genuinely differ. A number that will not say what it measures is decoration, not data.

Honesty

Separating what moves from what does not

The app trains achievement, which responds to practice, and rehearses ability formats, which builds familiarity rather than reasoning power. Every public claim is written to that distinction, including the ones that would sell better without it.

Privacy

Not collecting beats protecting

There is no account, no analytics and no network request. The reporting a product team would normally take for itself is computed on the device and shown to the family instead, which is the only party with a legitimate interest in it.

The line

What this program will and will not say.

“Practice moves achievement. It does not move a mental-ability score, and nothing honest claims otherwise. No app can promise a placement. What it can do is make sure that on test day, nothing on the page is the first of its kind your child has ever seen.”

That sentence is the reason the app caps mathematics one grade above a child’s own rather than advertising a bigger number, the reason the Mind Gym is described as format familiarity rather than IQ training, and the reason the eligibility page walks through the real rule rather than quoting the 99th percentile without saying which test it belongs to.

He could not buy it.
So he built it.

A father goes looking for a way to prepare his six-year-old. He finds a subscription library of worksheets, a phone number for a tutor, and a great deal of confident marketing about acceptance rates. What he cannot find anywhere, at any price, is a straight answer to the only question that matters: what exactly will my child be asked to do, and is he ready to do it?

So he built the thing he could not buy. Every family who opens The Gifted League gets it for the same reason his son did.

Dr Sam, founder

Who

A measurement person, first.

Dr Sam holds a PhD in statistics and research methods. His background is in applied statistics and quantitative research methods, with experience across epidemiology and analytics. He works on the kind of problems where a number has to be defensible, because somebody is going to act on it.

That is the whole reason this app exists in the form it does. Preparing a child for a screening test is a measurement problem wearing a parenting problem’s clothes. Once you see it that way, most of the design decisions make themselves.

Origin

It started with one child and one deadline.

The Gifted League began as a study aid for a single first grader named Samuel, built by his father in the months before gifted screening. The first version was small and specific. Ten worlds, each mapped to a real diagnostic domain and a real standards strand, so that a score referred to something. Everything read aloud, because a six-year-old who cannot yet decode a question independently is being measured on the wrong thing. And a game at the end that had to be earned, because the alternative was a parent standing over a child every evening.

It worked well enough on one child that it became worth building properly for everyone else’s. The first release landed on 20 July 2026. Kindergarten through fifth grade, the Mind Gym, Mock Test Day and the printable packs followed over the next three weeks, and the whole history is public.

The discipline

What a measurement person brings to a children’s app.

Mostly, a refusal to accept a number that has not been checked, and a habit of naming what an instrument can and cannot measure.

Item quality

Every question is a measurement

Distractors are built so that guessing on shape fails. The longest option and the shortest option are both wrong, numeric choices bracket the key rather than sitting all above or all below it, and any two options that would render identically on screen are removed before a question is ever shown.

Construct validity

A score has to refer to something

Each world states its diagnostic domain and standards strand in the parent panel, and the app reports mathematics and reading ceilings separately because they genuinely differ. A number that will not say what it measures is decoration, not data.

Honesty

Separating what moves from what does not

The app trains achievement, which responds to practice, and rehearses ability formats, which builds familiarity rather than reasoning power. Every public claim is written to that distinction, including the ones that would sell better without it.

Privacy

Not collecting beats protecting

There is no account, no analytics and no network request. The reporting a product team would normally take for itself is computed on the device and shown to the family instead, which is the only party with a legitimate interest in it.

The line

What this program will and will not say.

“Practice moves achievement. It does not move a mental-ability score, and nothing honest claims otherwise. No app can promise a placement. What it can do is make sure that on test day, nothing on the page is the first of its kind your child has ever seen.”

That sentence is the reason the app caps mathematics one grade above a child’s own rather than advertising a bigger number, the reason the Mind Gym is described as format familiarity rather than reasoning training, and the reason the program page walks through a real eligibility rule rather than quoting the 99th percentile without saying which test it belongs to.
